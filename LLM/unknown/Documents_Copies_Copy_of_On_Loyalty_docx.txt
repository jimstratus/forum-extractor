On Loyalty

Grand Moff / Admiral James Stratus (ret.) (SWCombine)



Loyalty is a faithfulness or a devotion to a person, country, group, or cause. Philosophers disagree as to what thing one can be loyal to. I have selected extracts from texts that have meaning to me, whilest some may be somewhat contradicting, I believe a balance of the below aspects merged together form the Stratus Doctrine On Loyalty:



Loyalty is a virtue, indeed a primary virtue, “the heart of all the virtues, the central duty amongst all the duties”. It is the basic moral principle from which all other principles can be derived. Loyalty is “the willing and practical and thoroughdoing devotion of a person to a cause.” The cause of which, must be objective and cannot be to one’s personal self. It is something external to oneself that one looks outward to the [universe] to find, and that cannot be found within. It concerns not one’s own person, but other people. The devotion is active, a surrendering of one’s self-will to the cause, that one loves. Loyalty is social. Loyalty to a cause unites the many fellow-servants of that cause, binding them together in their service. Loyalty is willing in that it is freely given, and not coerced. It is chosen after personal consideration, not something that one is born into. Loyalty is practical in that it is practiced. It is actively engaged upon, not passively expressed merely as a strong feeling about something.

-Josiah Royce, Philosophy of Loyalty. 1908 A.D.



	It is argued that the object of loyalty is always a person, though the objects of loyalty may encompass principles, causes, ideas,  ideals, beliefs, religions, ideologies, nations, governments, parties, leaders, families, friends, regions, racial groups, and indeed “anything to which one’s heart can become attached or devoted.”

	-Milton R. Konvitz, Encyclopaedia of the History of Ideas



Loyalty can be either exclusionary or non-exclusionary and can be single or multiple. Exclusionary loyalty excludes loyalties to other people or groups; whereas non-exclusionary loyalty does not. People may have single loyalties, to just one person, group, or thing, or multiple loyalties to multiple objects. Multiple loyalties can constitute a disloyalty to an object if one of those loyalties is exclusionary, excluding one of the others. This, however, is a special case. In general, the existence of multiple loyalties does not cause a disloyalty. One can, for example, be loyal to one’s friends, or one’s family, and still, without contradiction, be loyal to one’s religion, or profession.

	-Stephen Nathanson, Patriotism, morality, and peace. 1993 A.D.



Other dimensions of loyalty: Loyalties differ in basis, in strength in scope, in legitimacy, and in attitude.
